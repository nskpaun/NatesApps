Level 1 · 3 minutes

Ackoff in three minutes

Russell L. Ackoff (1919–2009) was an American organizational theorist, one of the founders of operations research, and one of the most quotable voices in systems thinking. He trained as an architect, earned a PhD in the philosophy of science at the University of Pennsylvania, and taught at Case Institute of Technology and later Wharton for decades. He also consulted for hundreds of organizations, Anheuser-Busch most famously.

Big idea 1 · We're changing ages

In his November 1993 talk at the Systems Thinking in Action conference, Ackoff argued that we're in the early stage of a shift as big as the Renaissance. We're moving from the Machine Age, which understands things by taking them apart, to the Systems Age, which understands things by seeing the larger whole they belong to.

Big idea 2 · A system is more than its parts

A system's essential properties belong to the whole, not to any part. No single part of a car can carry you across town, and if you take a car apart you no longer have a car, even if you keep every piece. As Ackoff put it in many talks, a system is not the sum of its parts but the product of their interactions.

Big idea 3 · Organizations are social systems

Managers often run companies as if they were machines (the owner's tool) or organisms (bodies that just want to survive and grow). Ackoff argued that a company is a social system: it has purposes of its own, its people have purposes of their own, and it sits inside a society with its own purposes. Treat it like a machine and it pushes back.

Big idea 4 · Leadership is an aesthetic act

Ackoff held that leadership is poorly understood because it is mainly an aesthetic function. A leader does two aesthetic things:

  • Creation (inspiration): offering a vision so attractive, so beautiful, that people want to pursue it.
  • Recreation (enjoyment): making the pursuit itself satisfying and fun, so people find it worth doing for its own sake.
Inspiration without implementation is provocation, not leadership. Implementation without inspiration is management or administration, not leadership.

Ackoff's distinction, as quoted in later writing on his leadership paper.

Why it matters

If Ackoff is right, most "fixes" fail because they improve parts instead of the whole. Most leaders also fail because they manage means without inspiring ends. "The righter we do the wrong thing," he liked to say, "the wronger we become."

Level 2 · The 1993 talk

From Mechanistic to Social Systemic Thinking

Russell Ackoff, Systems Thinking in Action conference (Pegasus Communications), November 1993. A condensed version was published in The Systems Thinker as "From Mechanistic to Social Systemic Thinking" (Ackoff, with Kellie Wardman). What follows is a guided, section-by-section study of the talk's argument in my own words. To read the article itself, see the Library tab.

The arc in one line: our worldview came from the Renaissance and built the Machine Age. Its assumptions have broken down, and a new Systems Age needs a different way of thinking: synthesis, expansionism, and seeing business as a social system.

1. Everyone has a worldview

Ackoff opens by saying that each of us carries a theory of reality, a worldview, that we absorbed growing up and rarely examine. Worldviews change only rarely, and we're living through one of those changes right now. He compares its scale to the move from the Middle Ages into the Renaissance.

Study question: what is one belief about how organizations "work" that you absorbed without ever choosing it?

2. How the Renaissance built the Machine Age worldview

Ackoff sketches the Middle Ages: short lives, high infant mortality, most people never travelling far from where they were born, and attention fixed on the afterlife. Contact between cultures, through the Crusades and the trade of the Italian city-states, reawakened interest in this world. Out of that came three beliefs that defined the modern mind:

  1. Complete understanding is possible. In principle we can understand everything. He recounts the 19th-century confidence that science would wrap up the universe within a few decades.
  2. Understanding comes through analysis. To understand something, take it apart down to indivisible elements, explain the parts, then assemble those explanations into an explanation of the whole.
  3. All relationships are cause and effect. A cause is necessary and sufficient for its effect.

3. What cause-and-effect thinking implied

Ackoff draws out three consequences of treating everything as cause and effect:

  • Explanation means finding the cause. Following causes back leads to a first, uncaused cause, which the era identified with God.
  • Explanation is environment-free. If the cause alone is sufficient, nothing else matters, so you can study a cause and its effect in isolation, as in a laboratory.
  • Determinism. Everything that happens was made to happen by something earlier. There's no room for chance or choice.

Newton's universe was the perfect expression: a closed, self-running mechanical clock. Ackoff then lays out a three-step argument that he says launched the Industrial Revolution. The universe is a machine created by God. Man is made in God's image. So man should create machines.

4. The Industrial Revolution and the Machine Age

Work came to mean applying energy to matter to transform it. Industrialization mechanized work by breaking it into the simplest possible tasks, which could then be done by machines or by people acting like machines. Frederick Taylor's task decomposition is the emblem. The factory became a network of elementary tasks, and the cost was the dehumanization of work.

Study question: where in your own work are people asked to behave like interchangeable parts?

5. The dilemmas that cracked the Machine Age

  • Free will: if everything is determined, choice is an illusion. Thinkers couldn't live with that.
  • Limits of knowledge: Heisenberg's uncertainty principle showed that some things can't be known completely, even in principle. Ackoff cites John Dewey's view that understanding is an ideal we approach but never reach.
  • Systems: in the mid-20th century, Norbert Wiener's cybernetics and Ludwig von Bertalanffy's general systems theory offered a different way of thinking about wholes.

6. The Systems Age: what a system is

A system is a whole made of two or more parts, where each part can affect the behavior of the whole, and none has an independent effect, because each part's effect depends on what the others are doing. The crucial consequence:

A system's essential properties are properties of the whole. No part has them, and when you take the system apart it loses them.

The car example: a car's essential property is carrying people from place to place. A wheel can't do that, nor an axle, nor a carburetor. Disassemble the car, keep every part, and you no longer have a car. So analysis, which takes things apart, can never reveal what makes a system a system.

7. Analysis vs synthesis; knowledge vs understanding

Analysis is still useful: it reveals structure, how a thing works. That's knowledge. But it can't explain why a system works the way it does. That's understanding, and it requires synthesis, which runs in three steps:

  1. Identify a larger system that contains the thing you want to understand.
  2. Explain the behavior of that containing whole.
  3. Explain the thing by its role or function within the whole.

The car example again: analysis tells a mechanic how a car works. Synthesis explains why American cars were designed to seat six: the car was a family vehicle, and the American family averaged about 5.6 people when those designs were set. As family size fell, cars shrank. You only see that by looking outward to the family and society, not inward at the engine.

Try it: to understand your team, don't start with its org chart. Start with the organization it serves, ask what that organization is trying to do, then ask what your team's role in it is.

8. Expansionism and producer–product

The Systems Age swaps the Machine Age doctrines for new ones:

  • Expansionism replaces reductionism. Understanding grows by expanding the system you look at. Complete understanding is never reached, but larger wholes give deeper understanding.
  • Producer–product complements cause–effect. Ackoff credits his teacher E. A. Singer Jr. for the idea. A producer is necessary but not sufficient for its product. An acorn is necessary for an oak, but throw it into the ocean or onto an iceberg and you get no tree. It also needs soil, water and light. So explanation becomes environment-full: you can't explain anything without its environment.
  • Because producers don't determine outcomes, choice and free will become compatible with scientific explanation. Cause–effect and producer–product are complementary views of the same world. Ackoff compares this to cutting an orange in different directions.

9. Business: machine, organism, or social system?

Ackoff traces how the business enterprise was conceived:

EraBusiness seen asWhat changed
Early Industrial RevolutionMachine: the owner's instrument, with no purpose of its ownThe owner was effectively the "god" of the firm. Workers, often barely surviving, did what they were told.
From the early 20th centuryOrganism: survival and growth are its purposesGrowth needed more capital than owners could reinvest, so ownership spread to shareholders and professional managers took over.
Post-WWII onwardSocial system: the whole, its parts and its environment all have purposesA better-educated workforce with safety nets was no longer driven only by survival. Movements for civil rights, women's liberation and youth showed the "parts" asserting their own purposes.

The trouble, Ackoff says, is that most managers still run social systems with machine or organism thinking.

10. The technology of the Systems Age

The Machine Age's signature technology was mechanization, machines that replace muscle by applying energy to matter. The Systems Age's signature is automation, machines that replace mind by observing, communicating and manipulating symbols. Ackoff traces the line from measuring instruments, through the telegraph, telephone, radio and television, to the electronic computer.

11. The closing warning

Our problems now arise in a world of social systems, yet we keep attacking them with mechanistic or organismic thinking. Until that mismatch is fixed, Ackoff warns, quality of life, and perhaps even standard of living, will keep eroding.

Level 3 · Leadership is aesthetic

Leadership as an aesthetic act

Ackoff's most surprising claim about leadership appears in "A Systemic View of Transformational Leadership" (Systemic Practice and Action Research, 1998) and related writing. He says leadership has been poorly understood largely because it is primarily an aesthetic function. To see why, you need his map of what people pursue.

The four ideals

Following the ancient Greeks, Ackoff names the ideals humans pursue. The Greeks identified three, and modern society added a fourth. Each has its own institution or field:

IdealPursued throughWhat it supplies
TruthScience and technologyInformation, knowledge, understanding
Plenty (abundance)EconomicsResources
The GoodEthics and moralityPeace, cooperation, the absence of conflict
BeautyAestheticsInspiration and enjoyment

Ackoff observed that organizations invest heavily in the first three: R&D, finance, compliance. Yet they barely think about the fourth, which is exactly where leadership lives.

Aesthetics has two faces

Creation: beauty that pulls you forward

The creative side of aesthetics is the sense of getting somewhere, of developing toward something worth having. A beautiful vision or ideal inspires: it makes people willing to give effort and to accept short-term sacrifice. This is why ideals matter even though they can never be fully reached. Their job is to pull.

Recreation: enjoyment along the way

The recreative side is the satisfaction we get from an activity itself, regardless of its outcome: play, fun, flow. Recreation re-creates us. It refreshes mind and body and restores energy for the long pursuit. Ackoff argued that work should carry this satisfaction too, which is why he cared so much about the quality of work life.

A leader is the person who supplies both: an inspiring end (creation) and a satisfying means (recreation). That's why Ackoff can call leadership an art. Like an artist, the leader works with "materials," here people, purposes and organizations, to produce something others find compelling.

Leader vs manager vs administrator

Inspiration without implementation is provocation, not leadership. Implementation without inspiration is management or administration, not leadership.

In Ackoff's terms (paraphrased), an administrator directs others toward ends using means that someone else selected. A manager directs others and selects the means, and perhaps the ends. A leader induces others to follow voluntarily. The key difference is voluntariness. You can order compliance, but you can only inspire commitment, and inspiration is an aesthetic act.

What a transformational leader does

Ackoff separates growth (getting bigger, having more) from development (increasing one's ability and desire to satisfy one's own needs and those of others). A transformational leader cares more about development. In his account, such a leader:

  1. Formulates, or facilitates the formulation of, an inspiring vision. Ideally it's co-created, so followers see their own purposes in it.
  2. Finds ways and means of pursuing it that are themselves satisfying.
  3. Implements, turning the vision into action and keeping it moving.
  4. Inspires and encourages others to pursue the vision voluntarily, and makes the pursuit enjoyable.

This links straight back to the 1993 talk. In a social system the parts are people with their own purposes. You can't make a social system work by command, as you would a machine. You have to align purposes, and that's what an inspiring, shared ideal does.

Can leadership be taught?

In later writing Ackoff argued that leadership is an art that requires talent. Like other arts it can be developed but not simply taught. A course can teach you about the materials and tools, as an art school teaches about paint, but not how to make something others find beautiful. That comes from practice, reflection and feedback.

An aesthetic audit of your own leadership

Use these questions on the plane. Score each from 1 to 5.

  • Creation: could everyone on my team state where we're going in a sentence, and would they find it beautiful, worth effort for its own sake?
  • Co-creation: did they help shape it, or did they receive it?
  • Recreation: do people enjoy the work itself? When did someone last describe a week as fun?
  • Voluntariness: if I stopped checking, would the work still get done because people want it done?
  • Development vs growth: are my people becoming more able, or are we just getting bigger?
If your scores are low on creation and recreation but high on execution, Ackoff would say you're managing, not leading.
Level 4 · The Ackoff toolkit

The Ackoff toolkit

The ideas Ackoff returned to across his career, all following from systems thinking.

Three (or four) kinds of system

Ackoff classified systems by purpose: whether the whole and its parts have purposes of their own.

TypeWhole has purpose?Parts have purpose?Example
Mechanistic (deterministic)No, it serves its user'sNoA car, a clock
Organismic (animate)Yes: survival and growthNo: your heart has no purpose of its ownA person, an animal
SocialYesYesA company, a university, a city
EcologicalNoYes (some)Nature, a market

The mistake Ackoff saw everywhere is managing a social system as if it were a machine (people as parts) or an organism (only survival and growth matter).

Messes, not problems

Ackoff argued that managers don't face independent problems. They face dynamic situations of interacting problems, which he called messes. Solving each problem separately usually makes the mess worse, because the problems interact. Manage the mess as a whole.

Four ways to treat a problem

TreatmentWhat you doTypical attitude
AbsolveIgnore it and hope it goes awayInactive
ResolveDo something "good enough," based on experience and common senseReactive, clinical
SolveFind the best possible answer, often through research and modelsOptimizing
DissolveRedesign the system so the problem can no longer ariseInteractive, the systems approach

Dissolving is Ackoff's favorite. The best way to handle a recurring problem is often to change the system that produces it.

Idealized design

Ackoff's signature method. Assume the system you're responsible for was destroyed last night, but its environment is untouched. What would you build in its place right now, if you could have anything that's technologically feasible and operationally viable? Design that ideal, then work backward to get as close as possible.

Ackoff traced the idea to a 1951 story about Bell Labs. A leader told researchers to assume the telephone system had been destroyed and to design the one they'd want instead. Innovations such as push-button phones are said to have followed. Idealized design is aesthetic leadership in practice. It creates a beautiful, shared ideal, and designing it together is energizing.

Interactive planning

Ackoff contrasted four planning stances: inactivism (keep things as they are), reactivism (go back to how things were), preactivism (predict the future and prepare for it), and interactivism (design a desirable future and invent ways to bring it about). Interactive planning has the people affected take part in designing it. Participation matters more than the plan.

Data → information → knowledge → understanding → wisdom

In 1989 Ackoff described a hierarchy that's now often drawn as the "DIKW pyramid":

  • Data: symbols, raw observations.
  • Information: data processed to answer who, what, where, when, how many.
  • Knowledge: answers how, the know-how that makes information usable.
  • Understanding: answers why, which takes synthesis.
  • Wisdom: evaluated understanding, judging what's worth doing, the effectiveness that values bring.

His jab was that education and management pour effort into the bottom layers and almost none into understanding and wisdom.

Efficiency vs effectiveness

Efficiency is how well you use resources to reach an objective; it's value-free. Effectiveness is efficiency weighted by the value of the objective. Doing the wrong thing efficiently is worse than doing the right thing badly, because you get further in the wrong direction.

The righter we do the wrong thing, the wronger we become.

Parts optimized ≠ whole optimized

In many lectures Ackoff told a story about building the best car by taking the best engine, the best transmission, the best brakes and so on from different manufacturers. You wouldn't get the best car. You wouldn't even get a car, because the parts don't fit together. Improving each part separately doesn't improve the whole, and can make it worse. What matters is how the parts interact.

Ackoff's books, if you want more

  • Redesigning the Future (1974): interactive planning.
  • Creating the Corporate Future (1981): planning as design.
  • Ackoff's Fables (1991): short, funny management stories.
  • Re-Creating the Corporation (1999): the social-systems view of the enterprise.
  • Idealized Design (2006, with Magidson and Addison).
  • Management f-Laws (2007, with Addison) and Systems Thinking for Curious Managers (2010): witty, unconventional truths about organizations.
Level 5 · Use it Monday

Use it on Monday

Five exercises that turn Ackoff's ideas into practice. Each takes 10–20 minutes with a notebook. A seat-back tray works fine.

1. Synthesis on your own role

  1. Name the larger system your team or job is part of.
  2. Write one sentence on what that whole is trying to do.
  3. Now explain your team by its role in that whole. Does today's work match that role?

2. Idealized design, 20-minute version

Assume your team (or product, or process) vanished last night. Its customers, market and constraints remain. On one page, design the version you'd build today, limited only by feasibility, not by the past. Then circle the three gaps between that design and reality that would matter most.

3. Map the mess

List the 5–8 problems currently stressing you. Draw arrows wherever solving one would make another better or worse. Look for the problem with the most arrows: that's your leverage point. Then ask whether any of them could be dissolved by a redesign instead of solved.

4. The aesthetic audit

Score your team from 1 to 5 on creation (is there an inspiring, shared vision?) and on recreation (is the work satisfying in itself?). For each, write one concrete thing you could do in the next two weeks to raise it by a point. Examples: co-write a one-sentence ideal with the team; kill a soul-draining recurring process; celebrate progress toward the vision, not just output.

5. Spot the machine thinking

Find one place where your organization treats people as interchangeable parts: a rule, a metric, a process. Ask what purpose those people bring to the work, and how the design could serve their purposes as well as the organization's.

Ackoff's test for any change: does it make the whole better, by improving how the parts interact, or does it just make one part look better?
