Light ribbon

The idea works, with one change to where the mirror goes. A mirror below an open band is dim, because a monitor sends very little light steeply downward. Put a 45° mirror directly in front of 12 mm of hidden rows instead, inside a slim sill, and a brushed cove under the display shows a ribbon of about 64 nits. That is clear in office light and vivid in the evening; beside a bright window only the paler colours still read. The whole optical path fits in 33 × 71 mm.

Why the layout as drawn is dim, and why it grew bulky

A monitor is brightest straight ahead. Measured IPS panels give about 43 % of their on-axis luminance at 45° and 24 % at 60°. A mirror under the display can only catch light that leaves the screen heading steeply down, and the closer the mirror sits to the display, the steeper that angle.

Moving the mirror forward fixes the angle and costs the space. A lip 95 mm in front of the screen reaches 22 nits; kept on the base it gets 3. In both, the mirror also faces the rest of the screen, so your windows leak into the ribbon.

The sill version turns the problem round. The mirror sits 0 to 12 mm in front of the pixels and takes the light the panel emits best. Those rows are hidden behind the sill, so the ribbon never sees your windows.

The compact layout, to scale

Side view of the selected layout. The grid is 10 mm. Change the layout with the buttons above.

  1. Hidden feed rows. The lowest rows of both panels sit behind the sill. RoseOS paints them; you never see them directly.
  2. 45° mirror in the sill. It turns their light straight down through a slot in front of the panel's chin.
  3. Mirror inside the sill's front wall. It folds forward-going light back under the display. Without it the ribbon loses about a fifth.
  4. Brushed cove. An ellipse with the slot at one focus and your eyes at the other, so the whole surface lights at once. Brushing runs along its length: that keeps the pattern sharp sideways and soft vertically.

A flat ramp in place of the curved cove gives the same brightness but a ribbon about half as tall (15 mm). A matte white cove would glow at 19 nits and wash out in office light, so the satin finish is doing most of the work.

Daylight: it depends on the colour

The ribbon competes with room light landing on the same cove. Luminance, not saturation, decides what survives daylight: amber carries about half of white's luminance, deep oxblood under a tenth. Rows are the selected layout at full band brightness.

Each cell is ribbon nits and how many times brighter that is than the cove surface without it. Tiers are a judgement call for a large, soft, coloured patch: under 0.05× invisible, to 0.2× faint, to 0.6× visible, to 2× clear, above that vivid. The room is modelled as an evenly bright surround; a window or lamp aimed straight into the cove would do worse, a shaded desk better.

How much screen it takes

Brightness climbs quickly up to about 12 mm of feed band and slowly after that. Ribbon height stops growing near 16 mm unless the cove also moves lower, which is what the Tall layout does. The hidden strip is the feed band plus the sill's 2.8 mm wall. Rows are for the measured brushed finish with the cove 34 mm below the slot.

Seat position

Ribbon nits for a white band as your eyes move. The cove is shaped for the outlined seat. Leaning in or slouching costs little; standing close is the weak corner.

Cove finish

The finish sets two things. Roughness along the ribbon decides how sharp the pattern stays sideways, so the brush should run along it. Roughness across the ribbon decides brightness and daylight contrast: a smoother cove sends more of the band's light to your eyes and less of the room's.

The first row uses roughness measured on brushed aluminium. A finer brush is 40 % brighter and holds 3× the contrast in office light. It costs a little at the edges of the seat range (20 nits against 25 standing close) and the ribbon starts to read as a reflection instead of a glow. Bead-blast spreads light every way and loses on both counts. This choice is best made with samples in hand.

What the ribbon can and cannot show

  • Colour and brightness along its length, in fine detail. Sideways blur is 7 mm at the centre and 10 mm at the ends, so even 10 mm highlights survive. Try the test bars above. Slow swells and small moving glints both work; softness is a software choice.
  • Not a wave shape. Height within the band barely maps to height in the ribbon. A pictured wave would have to come from brightness and colour moving along the ribbon, not from a line that rises and falls.
  • A dip at the seam and dim ends. The 14 mm seam shows as a soft notch. On a flat pair the outer 45 mm of each end fade, because the ribbon there looks for band that is past the panel's edge. Night Array's 10° toe-in trims that by about a fifth.
  • Sharp edges lean. The ribbon is an image a few centimetres behind the cove, seen at an angle, so a hard vertical edge tilts outward away from the centre. Smooth patterns hide it completely.
  • Not fully off in a dark room. An IPS panel's black still leaks about 1/1000 of white, which leaves the ribbon near 0.06 nits. Faintly visible in a blacked-out room, gone in any light.

What this rests on, and what to check on a bench

The tracer follows rays backward from the eye through the mirrors and a microfacet model of the satin surface to the panel or the room. It is checked against closed-form cases and against an independent forward photon count of the same layout (agreement within 3 %).

  • Panel: Samsung S60UD, 350 nits typical (280 minimum, which scales every ribbon number by 0.8). Angular falloff from two IPS monitors measured to 60°. The sill result moves by under 1 % across that measured range. The as-drawn base-mirror result moves by 30 %, because it lives on the steep tail where no measurement exists.
  • Mirror: 90 % reflectance, between acrylic mirror sheet and enhanced aluminium.
  • Cove: roughness fitted to measured brushed aluminium (0.05 along the brush, 0.14 across), 60 % reflectance with a warm tint. No measured data was found for bead-blast or PVD finishes; those rows are estimates.
  • Chin: 16 mm from the Night Array spec. The S60UD's real bottom border may be nearer 24 mm; that lengthens the slot by 8 mm, changes brightness by under 2 % and shortens the ribbon by about 4 mm.
  • Not modelled: polarisation. The panel's light is polarised and the direction is unknown; reflection off metal at these angles should change by a few percent either way.

A one-evening bench test would settle the two real unknowns, the finish and polarisation: a strip of mirror tile at 45° against the bottom of the actual monitor, a card as the sill, and a length of brushed aluminium angle below it, viewed from the seat with the room lights on.
